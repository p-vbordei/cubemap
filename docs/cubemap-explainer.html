cubemap takes how an organisation actually works — a conversation, an Excel, a receipt processed by hand — and turns it into functional requirements, a running simulation, and finally cubes: self-contained capability packages an AI coding app can implement.

chapter one · why builds fail

A sentence is a whole system in disguise.

A team rarely fails because the code was bad. It fails because nobody ever drew the business clearly enough for everyone — the client included — to point at it and say "yes, that's how it works." Take one ordinary moment and it unfolds into actors, steps, and a rule nobody wrote down.

chapter two · start at the top

Draw it with the people who run it.

The first altitude is the business itself. You run an Event Storming session on a shared canvas: the things that happen (past-tense events), the rules they live by, and the questions nobody can answer yet — all in the client's own words. The map is the artifact everyone can read, the boardroom and the engineer alike.

chapter three · the spine

One map. Four altitudes.

Like Google Maps for a system: stand at country level and see whole capabilities, then zoom to the street level of a single function. Each altitude takes the one above as its input, and you never add detail below that contradicts the picture above. One contract threads through all four — so a line of code always traces back to a business reason.

chapter four · fakes-first

You don't wait for the data.

This is the part most teams get wrong. A contract defines the shape of the data — and that shape alone is enough to generate plausible synthetic data and serve it from a fake. The moment a contract exists at the Meso level, the whole flow is clickable on day one — no real backend, no complete examples, no waiting.

chapter five · the loop

Every example makes it truer — and gets registered.

As real examples arrive — an Excel export, a blank form, a receipt — each one walks a node from coarse synthetic toward production-real, and the simulation converges on reality. Nothing is absorbed silently: every artifact is logged in an evidence register, so any part of the model traces back to the piece of client evidence that justified it.

chapter six · rules made physical

Illegal states simply can't be reached.

Business rules don't belong in scattered if statements a developer must remember. Modelled as statechart guards, a rule like "no refund before inspection" becomes something the system literally cannot violate. Compliance stops being a checklist and becomes the shape of the machine.

chapter seven · the endpoint

It ends in cubes, not a report.

The name is literal. A cube is one capability mapped all the way down — its story, its policies and slice of the lexicon, its contracts and fakes, its statecharts, and its registered evidence — one self-contained block. Because it speaks to its neighbours only through its contracts, it can be picked up and handed off on its own.

chapter eight · redeployment

Pick a cube up. Stand it back up — AI-native.

Hand a cube to an AI coding app with a one-sentence brief — "implement this against these contracts and statecharts; the acceptance criteria are your tests; change neither" — and it drops into the running system in place of its fake. Implement one cube, run another on its fakes a while longer, give a third to a different team. The organisation is redeployed, AI-native, one cube at a time.

chapter nine · who drives

Two ways to use it.

It runs from either side of the table. A consultant can drive it as a playbook with the client — or you can hand the skill to a non-technical department manager and say "talk to the AI until your processes are mapped." In that mode the AI becomes the facilitator: it leads, they answer, it shows the picture back, and it keeps going until the map is complete.

The map, the requirements, the simulation, and the system are four views of one thing, kept in sync. Draw before you specify; simulate before you build; let the client make it truer by feeding you what they have. The goal is always a system everyone can see, agree on, and trust before it's expensive to be wrong.
